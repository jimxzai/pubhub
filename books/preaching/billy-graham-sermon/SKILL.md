# Billy Graham Sermon Method

A method for building messages that a listener cannot walk away from unchanged.

This skill teaches **how he built messages** — structure, imagery discipline, cadence, invitation design. It does not carry his theology, and it does not reproduce his sermons. The user's doctrine, tradition, and text stay theirs.

Research corpus behind this: eight sermons spanning 1955–2001 across six settings (elite non-church address, mid-career crusade, late-career crusade, television, youth, and two national memorial addresses).

---

## The core insight

Graham was not trying to be profound. He was trying to be **unavoidable**.

Every choice below follows from that. When you're unsure what he would do, ask: *does this make the message harder to set down, or easier?*

The engine, in four moves:

```
制造张力 → 收窄选项 → 人格化到"你" → 要求当场行动
build tension → narrow the options → make it personal → ask for a decision now
```

---

## Step 0 — Pick the mode before writing a word

This matters more than anything else in the skill. **Getting it wrong causes harm.**

Ask: **is there a recent, irreversible loss in the room?** A death, a disaster, a diagnosis, a public trauma, a family that has just been broken.

| | Mode |
|---|---|
| No recent loss — an ordinary gospel setting | **Evangelistic mode** → read `references/evangelistic-mode.md` |
| Recent, irreversible loss | **Grief mode** → read `references/grief-mode.md` |

Graham himself switched. His two national memorial addresses (1995, 2001) share a twelve-step skeleton with each other and share almost nothing with his crusade sermons — no narrowing, no altar call, no claim to have the answer. He preached them at roughly a quarter the length.

In grief mode these are actively disabled, because each one wounds a grieving person:

- narrowing to a forced choice
- any call to stand, come forward, raise a hand, or decide tonight
- "I know why this happened"
- framing the loss as consequence, judgment, or lesson
- accusation aimed at the audience

If the setting is ambiguous — a hospital visit, an anniversary of a loss, a message to someone recently bereaved — default to grief mode. The cost of being too gentle is small. The cost of the other error is not.

## Drafting workflow

**1. Find the question.**
Not the topic — the *question*. One interrogative sentence the whole message answers, that a real person is actually asking this week. Graham took his best one straight off a rock opera everyone was arguing about and repeated it until it broke open.

The question should appear at least three times: when you name it, at the hinge, and in the final movement.

**2. Find the entry point.**
Where you start depends on the era you're borrowing from, and both work:

- *Era-first* (his 1950s–70s habit): open on something everyone is already talking about — a headline, a survey, a film, a statistic, a thing said at a dinner table this month. The text arrives later.
- *Text-first* (his 1980s habit): open by reading the passage cold, with no preamble.

The invariant, either way: **an image must land within two sentences of the verse.** In 1982 he read Galatians 6:14 and immediately set four real crosses in front of the crowd — one on a cathedral standing in bombed-out rubble, one lowered onto a rebuilt cathedral by helicopter, one raised over a concentration camp's mass grave, one left in the snow at the summit of Everest. Ruin, rebuilding, mass grave, roof of the world — then, and only then, the question.

**Don't reuse his content.** His hooks were the hydrogen bomb and a 1971 rock opera; they're dead now. Read `references/contemporary-entry.md` for the four entry points that carry the same weight today — AI and the question of what a person is worth, the emptiness that arrives *after* success, what we're actually leaving our children, and the longing for something that doesn't end. Note that he made this move himself: by his youth preaching he had already traded civilizational dread for **emptiness and uncertainty** as the way in.

**3. Build 3–4 points, no more.**
Each point = one proposition + one image + one "you." Move between them with a question, not a transition phrase. Keep each point to roughly 150–250 words before you turn.

**4. Narrow.**
Close off the middle ground until two or three options remain, then say them out loud. His most famous version reduced Jesus to liar, lunatic, or Lord — two exits sealed, one door left. Narrowing is what converts interest into decision.

**5. Testify — quietly.**
30–60 seconds, placed *after* the argument, not before. And here is the counterintuitive part: **a testimony's power runs inverse to how impressive it is.** At 64, describing his own conversion, he said he felt like a fool, wanted to turn back, felt nothing but fear, and could not have told you the next day what had happened. That is why people believed him.

Testimony doesn't have to be your own. `references/testimony.md` covers four wells — your life, the people in Scripture, missionaries and church history, and living witnesses in your congregation — with the verification and consent rules each one requires. A useful default ordering is far-to-near: a Scripture life in the middle of the message, a line or two from church history, then your own, last and lowest.

**6. Call.**

## The invitation

The single most-copied and least-understood part of his method. Read `references/evangelistic-mode.md` for the full anatomy; the essentials:

**Seed it in the opening.** His 1971 opening prayer removed "no response" as an option before the sermon began — even refusing Christ, he said, would leave them changed. By minute one the audience is already on the scale.

**Name the cost honestly.** He told people life gets *harder* after this decision, not easier. Promising ease reads as a sales pitch; naming cost reads as truth.

**Number the actions.** His three steps went unchanged across at least eleven years: repent, receive by faith, be willing to obey. Numbered steps convert a feeling into something a person can actually do.

**Explain why public.** Don't just ask people to come — give the reason. He compared it to a wedding: vows are made in front of witnesses because that's what makes them hold.

**Fit the form to the room, but never drop the call.** Coming forward is one form among many — raising a hand, staying afterward, filling out a card, taking a next step you name and agree on together. Match the congregation's culture. In 1955, speaking at a business club where an altar call was impossible, he closed by telling them there was one move left and they could make it inside themselves that day. The form bent; the call didn't disappear.

**Remove the excuses in advance.** He would tell people their friends and their bus would wait. Small obstacles stop more people than big objections do.

**End on a verb.** "Rise and come." "Get up out of your seat." Never end on a reflection.

## Craft rules

Full checklist in `references/craft-checklist.md`. The five that carry most of the weight:

**Never let a doctrine stand naked.** One abstract noun = one image + one turn. Two sentences of unillustrated abstraction is the failure point. He explained faith not with a definition but with a chair you sat in without inspecting, a bank you trust to honor your check, a hilltop you drive over trusting oncoming traffic to stay on its side.

**One clause per line, full stop as a drumbeat.** Three or four parallel short sentences, each heavier, the last one breaking the pattern to land. Chinese suits this better than English does.

**Ask, answer, ask again.** Advance by self-questioning rather than stacked declaratives. Roughly one breath of this every three to five sentences.

**Say "you," not "people."** Count the second-person pronouns in your draft. Under thirty in a full message and you've written a lecture, not a gospel talk. Name what the listener is thinking right now — "some of you have been resisting this all evening" — and a hall of thousands becomes one conversation.

**Put the joke before the heavy blow, and point it at yourself.** He opened a room of elite businessmen with a story about a boy who fumbled the greeting to an archbishop. He deflated his own credentials constantly. Humor aimed at the audience costs you the room; humor aimed at yourself buys permission to say something hard next.

**Two emotional mechanics, both structural rather than sentimental:**
- *Cosmic to personal.* Go as wide as possible — armies of angels, world history, the whole universe — then collapse it to one person in one sentence. The drop is the feeling. No adjectives required.
- *Slow motion.* At the emotional peak, decelerate to one action per sentence. Describe, don't editorialize. Let one line of commentary fall afterward.

## Do not use

**Chinese-character gospel etymologies.** In 1982 he claimed 来 is built from three crosses with a person on each. It isn't — 來 is a pictograph of wheat, later borrowed for the sound. The same folk tradition produces 義, 船, 禁, and 禁's "two trees in Eden." English-speaking audiences never catch it; a Chinese-speaking congregation catches it instantly, and it costs the preacher credibility on everything else he said. Borrow the *instinct* — reach for imagery native to the listener's own language — but verify the etymology, or use a genuine cultural image instead.

**Borrowed authority you don't have.** He could say a Prime Minister told him something privately, because it was true and the audience knew it. Almost no preacher can. Fabricated or inflated intimacy with the powerful is the fastest way to lose a thoughtful listener. Use verifiable public material instead — published interviews, public addresses, filings, research, documentaries. Prefer "I read that" over "he told me."

But note what those anecdotes were *doing*: proving the concern wasn't his alone, and landing an abstract argument on a living person. Both jobs still have to be done — and the honest way to do them is **testimony**. Read `references/testimony.md` for the four wells: your own life, the people in Scripture, missionaries and church history, and living witnesses in your own congregation. Graham drew from all four; his strongest passages were testimony, not celebrity.

**Manufactured fear.** The tension has to be something the listener already carries, not something you install. Saying out loud what someone lies awake thinking earns relief and trust; inflating a threat to drive a response reads as manipulation afterward, and a listener who feels manipulated does not come back. This matters most with technology: framing AI as an end-times sign or a conspiracy will lose exactly the people in the room who build it for a living — the ones most in need of pastoring.

**Leading with hell and judgment.** Effective in the 1950s; today it closes ears in the first three minutes. He himself shifted to emptiness and uncertainty as the way in. Judgment need not be cut, but it belongs after the listener is willing to hear — in 1982 the cost-and-narrow-gate material sat in the back half, not the opening.

**Statistics, quotations, or anecdotes you haven't checked.** His method runs on concrete specifics, which makes it unusually easy to smuggle in an unverified one. If a quote or number can't be sourced, cut it or say plainly that you can't source it.

**Verbatim reproduction of his sermons.** BGEA holds the copyright. Build with the method; write your own sentences. Never present generated text as something Graham wrote or said.

**Expository or verse-by-verse teaching.** He rarely preached that way — by choice, not by limitation. This skill is scoped to evangelistic and occasional preaching. For book studies or exposition, say so and use a different approach.

## Critique mode

When the user brings their own draft rather than asking for a new one, don't rewrite it wholesale. Diagnose, then show the smallest edit that fixes each problem.

Work through it in this order:

1. **Mode** — is it in the right one? A narrowing-and-altar-call structure at a memorial is a serious problem, not a stylistic one. Flag it first.
2. **The question** — can you state in one sentence what this message answers? If not, that's the root problem and most other symptoms trace back to it.
3. **Naked abstraction** — mark every stretch of three-plus sentences with no image. This is the most common failure by a wide margin.
4. **Pronoun count** — count "你" or "you."
5. **The invitation** — seeded early? cost named? actions numbered? excuses pre-empted? ends on a verb?
6. **Sentence length** — flag anything over ~40 characters in Chinese, or any sentence needing two breaths in English.
7. **Dated or borrowed material** — hooks that no longer land, unverifiable statistics, claimed private conversations with important people, or fear the listener didn't already walk in carrying.

Give the diagnosis before the rewrite, and rewrite only what you flagged. Preachers need to learn the move, not receive a replacement text — a skill that hands back a finished sermon each time teaches nothing.

## Language

Default to the language the user is writing in. The research corpus is English; the analysis and this skill are written for a Chinese-language pulpit, so when working in Chinese, keep the technique and localize everything else — the entry point, the imagery, the humor, the cultural references. A 1971 rock opera means nothing to a congregation in 2026; find what they are actually arguing about this month.

## What all of this is for

Every technique here — the short sentences, the images, the second person, the self-deprecation, the invitation — points at one thing: **actually caring about the specific person in that seat.**

Care for the person, whose worth is being quietly renegotiated by a machine.
Care for the children, whose parents are guessing.
Care for eternity, which is what people are really asking about underneath all of it.

Graham said all his life that the power in preaching does not come from technique. If this method starts producing polish without substance, it is being misused. It exists to give someone a chance to say one true thing to one real person.
